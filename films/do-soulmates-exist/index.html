The question

You have known Sam for forty minutes, and you are already fairly sure that Sam is the one. You met in the kitchen of a birthday party where you knew almost nobody. Sam was in there too, guarding a bowl of peanuts. You made a small joke about the peanuts, the kind you normally keep to yourself, and Sam laughed before you had finished it. Since then you have found out that you both eat the crusts first, that you were both frightened of the same cartoon rabbit, and that neither of you can whistle. It has been forty minutes, and it feels like years.

Walking home, you think what people have thought on nights like this for a very long time: this person was made for me. Under that thought sits a rule most of us never say out loud. When we meet the right person, we will simply know.

Three years later, it is a Tuesday. Sam is telling you the plot of a film you have both seen, and getting it wrong, and you catch yourself wondering whether the person who was made for you would really tell it like this. Nothing has happened. The feeling from the kitchen has simply not come round this evening, and the feeling was supposed to be the proof.

Is there one person meant for each of us, and will we know them when we meet?

The film answers in about five minutes. Here there is room for more of the two old stories it borrows, and for the research behind each step. Where this page goes further than the stories and the studies do, the step is the film’s.

The other half

The film’s first story comes from Plato’s Symposium, a book about a dinner party in Athens where the guests take turns to make speeches in praise of love. The comedian at the table is Aristophanes, a real comic poet. When his turn comes he has hiccups, and someone else has to go first. He does not mind saying something funny, he says, since that is his trade. He minds being laughed at.1

Once, he says, people were round. Each of us had four arms, four legs and two faces on one neck, and we got about by cartwheeling on all eight limbs. There were three kinds: male, female, and a third that was half man and half woman. The round people tried to climb up to heaven and attack the gods. Zeus did not want to destroy them, because then the gods would get no more sacrifices, so he cut each one in two. Your navel, in the story, is where the skin was drawn together over the cut.

Ever since, each half has longed for the half it was cut from. Because there were three kinds of round person, the story has room for every kind of couple: men who love women, women who love men, women who love women and men who love men.

The first halves to find each other did not live happily ever after. They held on to each other, wanting to grow back into one, and began to die of hunger and neglect, “because they did not like to do anything apart”, as Benjamin Jowett translated it in 1892. So Zeus took pity on them and changed their bodies, so that two people could make love, be satisfied, and then go back to their work.

That, he says, is what each of us is.

When someone does meet their own other half, he goes on, the two will not be apart even for a moment. Such pairs spend their whole lives together, and still “they could not explain what they desire of one another.” Suppose Hephaestus, the god of the forge, made them an offer: “I am ready to melt you into one and let you grow together, so that being two you shall become one”. Not one of them would say no. That, says Aristophanes, is love: “the desire and pursuit of the whole”.

Near the end comes the part the film left out. Aristophanes admits that few people now find their own true half, and that the best open to us is “the attainment of a congenial love”: someone who suits us.

Later that evening Socrates repeats what a woman named Diotima once taught him (scholars disagree about whether she lived), and it answers Aristophanes directly: lovers are seeking “neither for the half of themselves, nor for the whole, unless the half or the whole be also a good.” Aristophanes starts to reply, and a drunken knocking at the door cuts him off. He never gets his answer in.

So the other half is Aristophanes’ story: a comedian’s speech, full of jokes and real longing, which Plato wrote and had another guest answer. The word “soulmate” is nowhere in it. It is English, and the Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for it is from 1822, in the writing of Samuel Taylor Coleridge.2

The tally

When the comedian says what each of us is, he uses the Greek word symbolon (σύμβολον). The dictionary of ancient Greek gives it as a “tally”: an object two people broke in half when they made a promise, each keeping a piece, so that one day the pieces would fit and prove who the other was.3

In the story, the halves are made by Zeus’s cut. The word the comedian chooses for them comes from something people did: two of them made a promise, and each kept a piece. The film ends on that kind of joining, a cord two people tie themselves, and the link between that ending and the word is the film’s own.

The red cord

The film’s second story is a tale from Tang China by Li Fuyan (李復言), called 定婚店 (Dinghun dian), which Douglas Wilkerson translates as “The Inn of Betrothal”. It survives in two old collections, 續幽怪錄 and 太平廣記, which disagree about the year it happens.4 The story goes like this.

A young man named Wei Gu wants to marry, and every match he tries comes to nothing. On a journey he stops at an inn south of the town of Songcheng, to meet a matchmaker. Before dawn, while the moon is still bright, he comes upon an old man reading a book by moonlight, with a cloth bag beside him. It is a book of the unseen world, the old man says, and as for his work, in Wilkerson’s English: “I’m in charge of all the marriage contracts on earth.”5

The match Wei has come for will fail, the old man says. His wife is three years old now, and she will come to him when she is seventeen. Wei asks what is in the bag.

The old man points her out in the market: a little girl carried by a poor, one-eyed old woman who sells vegetables. Wei is furious. He sharpens a small knife, gives it to his servant, and offers him ten thousand copper coins to kill the child. The servant stabs at her heart, and strikes her between the eyebrows instead.

For fourteen years every match Wei tries fails. Then a governor gives him a young woman of his family in marriage. She always wears a little flower ornament between her brows, and after a year Wei presses her to tell him why. She was brought up by her nurse, she says, who sold vegetables, and when she was three a madman stabbed her in the market. Wei tells her it was he who had her stabbed. It is strange, he says; it is fate. From then on the two of them hold each other in even greater respect. Near its end the tale gives its lesson, 乃知隂騭之定不可變也: so we know that what is settled in secret cannot be changed (our translation). The inn is renamed the Inn of Betrothal.

The tale calls him only “an old man”. Later tradition calls him the Old Man under the Moon (月下老人, Yuexia Laoren, or 月老, Yue Lao, for short), and people still pray to him for love.6 In the tale the cord ties the feet. We could not find where the version with the little finger began.

The old man ties the cord when the two are born, and the tale’s lesson is that nothing anyone does can undo it. Its hero does not know his wife when he sees her, he tries to have her killed, and the cord holds anyway. The film borrows the cord and leaves the tale its own lesson. By the film’s end the cord has become one that two people tie themselves, one small knot at a time: a change the film makes to the tale.

What the research found, and where it stops

For most of these studies only the published summary could be read, so some numbers are missing.

Believing in the one

Some people believe love is meant to be: two people are right for each other, or they are not. Others believe love is made. One researcher followed students through their relationships.7 For the students who believed love is meant to be, how happy they felt at the start told more about how long the relationship would last than it did for the others. When things got difficult, these students were more likely to look away from the problem.

Another study asked students which they believed, and went back to some of them eight months later.8 For those who believed love is meant to be, feeling that they had found their ideal partner counted for more than it did for the rest, both for how happy they were and for whether they were still together.

Then the same team ran two experiments. For a short while, they led some people to believe that love is meant to be, and others that it is made.9 In the first group, people who felt they were with the right person read what happened in the relationship’s favour, and people who doubted it read it against the relationship. In the second group, neither leaned much either way. These are the only experiments we found that give people the belief and watch what it does.

So the belief cuts both ways. Believers who feel they have found the right person do well, and those who doubt it do worse. The Tuesday when Sam gets the plot wrong, and it starts to look like a sign, is the film’s picture of that second group.

People who believe love is made seem to cope better when things are hard. In two more studies, they minded less when a partner fell short of their ideal, and an argument shook their commitment less. People who leaned the other way were more hostile when they and their partner talked about where they differed.1011

None of this means that believers are unhappier. The largest study we found followed about nine hundred couples for two years.12 The more strongly people believed love is meant to be, the more satisfied they were at the start. The more strongly they believed it is made, the more slowly their satisfaction fell afterwards.

We found no review that puts these studies together to see how large the effects are. Many of the people studied were students, and most of the studies show only that two things go together.

Why a stranger can feel familiar

In one study, people read short descriptions of strangers, and some of the strangers resembled someone important in the reader’s own life.13 Afterwards, people “remembered” things about those strangers that had never been said, things that were true of their own important person.

Later studies found this happened without any prompting. A stranger who resembled someone the reader felt warmly about made their expression warmer, and made them want to be close.1415

The strangers were only on paper, and nobody measured whether they felt familiar. Nearly all of this work comes from two labs, and we found no one else who has repeated it. So the film says only that a stranger can feel familiar this way.

Close within the hour

Two students in a psychology class, strangers to each other, sit down with 45 minutes and a list of 36 questions. They take turns asking and answering, and each question is a little more personal than the last. That is what one study asked of its pairs, and at the end they felt closer than pairs who had spent the time on small talk.16 A later study found the same, face to face or by video.17

The questions are often passed round as a way to fall in love. The studies measured only how close people felt at the end of the session, and we found none that shows the questions make anyone fall in love.

What a first evening cannot tell

Before an evening of four-minute speed dates, researchers measured more than a hundred things about each person: what they were like, and what they wanted in a partner.18 Then they gave it all to a computer program built to find patterns. It could tell them something about who tended to like people, and who tended to be liked. It could not tell them which two would take to each other. The study looked only at first attraction.

What grows in long couples

Couples do not slowly turn into each other. Married couples followed for twenty years stayed about as alike in personality as they were at the start.19

Their emotions do grow alike. One study followed dating couples and room-mates for a year, and over that year the emotional reactions of the two in each pair became more alike. The pairs whose emotions were more alike were closer, and less likely to split up.20 In dating couples and newlyweds, the couples whose emotions were alike, or grew alike, were the more satisfied.21

The film’s couple of forty years, who each know what the other’s silence means, are the film’s own picture, built on these findings. The studies watched emotions grow alike over a year or so; nobody measured silences, or forty years. And no study joins all of these into one story that runs from a kitchen to a Tuesday: that story is the film’s.

It takes two

The film says where its idea stops: “It does take two people tying. No amount of effort from one side makes a pair, and nobody owes more knots to someone who treats them badly. Some first evenings are only good evenings, and some loves are right to end.”

Among women at a shelter, those with fewer places to go were more committed to the partner who had abused them, and the committed were more likely to go back.22 Newly married couples were followed for four years. Where one partner was more ready to forgive, the other’s aggression carried on as before; where they were slower to forgive, it fell.23 Forgiving someone who had not made amends lowered people’s self-respect.24 And in marriages followed over years, the gentle habits, such as forgiving and hoping for the best, went with a marriage doing better where it was healthy, and doing worse where it was troubled.25

We found no study that tests directly whether believing that love is made keeps people in harmful relationships. The evidence points that way, but only indirectly.

The things that keep a good relationship going can keep someone in a harmful one. So the film’s idea that love is made holds where both people are safe, and both are making it.

Made by two

The film ends years later, in another kitchen, with the two of you telling the story of how you met, so that it sounds as though it could never have gone any other way. That scene is the film’s own. The nearest study we found asked something else. It asked partners, again and again, how their love had changed since the last time they were asked. Those who stayed together said each time that it had grown, although their scores on the same questions had not generally risen.26

None of the stories or studies above asked whether soulmates exist, so the answer at the end belongs to the film alone.

The feeling in the kitchen was real, and a good deal of it was yours. You brought it in with you: the people you have loved, your crusts, your cartoon rabbit, your honest opinion of peanuts. If you have lost someone and feared the feeling went with them, it was yours before you met them, and it is still yours now.

So, back to the Tuesday. Sam is still getting the plot wrong. Sam has also, without being asked, made your tea exactly the way you take it. Nobody else alive can do that. Three years ago this was a stranger who laughed at a joke about peanuts, and now it is somebody who knows how you take your tea. That is a knot, and there are more of them than you had noticed.

So yes, soulmates exist. They are made, slowly, by two people, one ordinary Tuesday at a time, and the making can begin in any kitchen.
