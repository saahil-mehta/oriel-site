The question

A friend cancels on you an hour before dinner. The message is kind, it says sorry, and it ends with a little heart. It still stings. You had kept the evening free and you were looking forward to it. Somewhere in you a small verdict forms: they just couldn’t be bothered.

Last week you cancelled on someone yourself, and that felt completely different. You had reasons, good ones, and anyone who knew your week would have understood. You sent a kind message with a little heart, and you meant the heart.

Why does the same message feel reasonable when we send it, and careless when it comes to us?

The film answers in four and a half minutes. This article takes the same answer more slowly. It gives the research behind it, what each study tested and where it stops, a line Seneca wrote in the first century AD, and what helps.

Inside and outside

The film tells it with two Fridays. On the first, you have had a week of a dead boiler, an official envelope you are not ready to open and two missed buses. You cancel dinner with Nina from the sofa and add a heart. On the next Friday, Nina cancels on you with the same words and the same heart, and you decide straight away that she couldn’t be bothered. Then the film crosses the street and goes into her house. Her landlord is selling the flat, her cat is wearing a plastic cone, and there is a tooth she is trying not to think about. At a quarter to seven she stood in her hallway with one arm in her coat for a long time, and then she wrote to you.

Her week was as full as yours. But you could only see one of them, your own. You know your own week from the inside: the cold showers, the envelope on the hall table, how tired you were at seven o’clock. Other people’s weeks you see from the outside, by what they did. The message arrived and the dinner was off, and that is all you had.

The film draws each person as a house. Yours is lit in every room, and every reason is exactly where you left it. Everyone else shows you a front door and a drawn curtain. So we judge ourselves with all our reasons in view, and a friend by the one thing we saw them do.

Nina had the same view of you. On your Friday she could not see your boiler or your envelope. She was already at the restaurant, with a basket of bread and a waiter asking whether she would like to keep waiting, when your message arrived. Your reasons were all true, and she still walked home in the rain.

What the research found, and where it stops

The film draws on five pieces of research. None of them looked at a cancelled dinner, so here is what each one did test, in whom, and what it found.

How we explain our own let-downs

In 2004 Amy Mezulis, Lyn Abramson, Janet Hyde and Benjamin Hankin pooled 266 studies with 503 separate results.2 Each study looked at how people explain the good and bad things that happen to them. People tended to give themselves the credit for good outcomes and to put bad ones down to circumstances. The average difference was large by the usual measure (d = 0.96), and it appeared in nearly all the samples.

Its size changed with culture. It was much smaller in Asian samples (d = 0.30) than in samples from the United States (1.05) or other Western countries (0.70). That is why the film says “most of us” and never “everyone”. The review compares how people explain their own successes with how they explain their own failures. Judging ourselves against other people is a separate question, which the next studies take up.

What we meant, and what they saw

Justin Kruger and Thomas Gilovich published five studies in 2004 on good intentions.3 In the first two, people gave themselves more credit for their good intentions than they gave other people for theirs. In the other three, this led people to rate themselves more favourably than they rated others. So we count what we meant, and give other people less credit for what they meant.

In 2006 Kruger, Cameron Gordon and Jeff Kuban studied teasing.4 People who tease usually try to show they are only joking, with a look or a tone of voice. The people being teased were often unaware of those good intentions, and unmoved by them. The teasers tended to see the teasing as innocent and playful, and the people teased saw it as much more unkind.

Both papers share a lead author, and the research for the film found no independent repeat of the five intention studies. They are what the film rests on when it says that all Nina had to go on was what you did.

The one who cancels and the one who waits

In 1990 Roy Baumeister, Arlene Stillwell and Sara Wotman asked people to write two true stories.5 One was about a time someone made them angry, and one about a time they made someone else angry. Writing as the one who caused it, people described what they had done as meaningful and understandable, a single incident that was now over. Writing as the one who was hurt, they described the other person’s act as pointless or hard to understand, part of a longer history, with harm that lasted. Several findings fitted the authors’ suggestion that the one who causes the hurt sees only the single incident, and takes the other person’s anger as an overreaction. In the film, the evening was over for you the moment you pressed send, and for Nina it went on a little longer.

The 1990 study used stories people remembered. The two kinds of story may be about different events, so it cannot show that the role alone changes how a story is told.

In 1997 Stillwell and Baumeister tested exactly that.6 Everyone read the same story, some as the person who did the harm and some as the person harmed. Then each retold it in their own words. Across three experiments, both sides changed the story about as much as each other, and more than people who had taken neither side, mostly by leaving things out. Those who did the harm added to their excuses and to the kind things they had done, and left out the facts that showed how bad it was and whose fault it was. Those who were harmed dwelt on how bad it had been.

Where the evidence stops

A broader claim is often made: that we put our own failings down to the situation and other people’s down to their character. Bertram Malle’s 2006 review of 173 studies found that difference close to zero on average (average effects from d = −0.016 to 0.095).7 It did hold in some conditions, among them bad events and people who know each other well, which is the case in this film. So the dinner is an illustration of something that can happen between friends, and the article claims no general law.

A second claim is even closer to this film’s question: that we judge the very same unfair act more kindly when we do it ourselves. A small experiment by Piercarlo Valdesolo and David DeSteno found this in 2007.8 A preregistered repeat with more than 600 people, by Claire Robertson, Madison Akles and Jay Van Bavel in 2024, did not find it.9 What the evidence does support is narrower: we know our own reasons, we count our own intentions, and we see other people by what they did.

Here the article goes beyond its sources. Each study looked at one piece: how people explain success and failure, intentions, teasing, and stories about anger. Putting them together as one account of a cancelled dinner is the film’s step, and ours.

An old line for it

Seneca, a Roman Stoic of the first century AD, wrote On Anger in three books, addressed to Novatus. The second book begins by asking whether anger starts by choice or by impulse, and then turns to practical ways of keeping from it. Chapter 28 opens with a condition: if we want to be fair judges of everything, we must first accept that none of us is without fault. A few lines later comes this sentence.

In plain words: when someone offends you, remember the times you were suspected of something you did not do. Remember when a good turn of yours looked like a wrong, and the people you disliked at first and came to love. Then say quietly to yourself, “I have done this very thing myself,” and you will be slower to anger.

Seneca’s list fits the film closely. A good turn that bad luck made look like a wrong is your own Friday, seen from the restaurant: true reasons that looked, from outside, like not bothering. The film takes his last words as a thought and says it in its own words. When you are the one left at the table, you know what a message like Nina’s can have behind it, because you sent one last Friday.

Seneca was writing about anger at real wrongs, among them insults, betrayals and theft. A cancelled dinner is a far smaller thing, and the film borrows his sentence for it.

What helps

The film’s answer is small and practical. When a friend lets you down, try the kinder guess first: think about what their week might have held. You know what a message like theirs can have behind it, because you have sent one yourself. Then ask. You cannot see a friend’s week from outside, and they can tell you about it.

In the film the asking goes both ways. You hear about the landlord and the tooth, and at some length about the cat. Nina hears about the boiler, and tells you to open the envelope. The next Thursday you get to the restaurant first, guard the bread, and watch the door until she comes in, one arm already out of her coat.

The film also says where this stops. Some weeks really do need the sofa, and nothing here says you should have dragged yourself out. And some people really are careless with us. The kinder guess is for people who mostly turn up for each other, where it costs very little to try it first.

None of the studies above tested asking. The kinder guess and the question are the film’s suggestion.

Everyone you know has a week you haven’t heard about. Ask them how it was.
